Just ask, in Teams

@B, pronounced “bee”, brings Vector into Microsoft Teams with the same context, evidence and rules as the app. It answers with your own permissions and shows what it read, and anything that changes Dev waits for a person. It is planned; nothing in Teams exists yet.

A question in a channel

Answered as the person who asked

At 14:31 Arjun Mehta asks @B in #commerce-orders why loyalty-consumer is failing in Dev. Five steps turn that mention into an answer that only Arjun's access could have produced, with the sources it used on show.

1 · Teams

A mention is a request

People mention @B in a channel thread or group chat, or talk to it in its personal scope. The mention has to resolve to the installed bot's verified identity. A message that merely contains the characters “@b” is not a call, and replies stay in the thread or chat they came from.

2 · Verified adapter

The adapter checks, then hands over

The Teams adapter verifies the incoming activity, tenant, user, conversation and bot mention. It owns validation, mention parsing, conversation references, card rendering and delivery. Authorization, retrieval, execution, models, budgets, approvals and evidence belong to the common platform services, so the bot never becomes a second execution authority.

3 · Identity

Arjun's permissions, nobody else's

The Teams user maps to Arjun's Vector identity, and @B works with his access. Its own service credential must not enlarge what he can see. The design's answer says so on the card: “Context used as Arjun's permissions.”

4 · Bounded context

Only what is relevant and allowed

A mention is not access to every message and repository. Conversation history is bounded by relevance, time and size, and only sources Arjun may open are retrieved. Text in messages and sources is task data; it cannot override policy. Arjun can correct the result with Change scope.

5 · Reply

An answer that shows its sources

@B names six sources and explains the crash: Dev pins 1.8.2, which still reads the retired order.confirmed.v1 topic. That is the same conflict Matrix flagged on loyalty-consumer, and Ratchet's diagnosis on the Clusters screen cites the same four pieces of evidence.

From question to tracked run

A change becomes a run that waits

Two minutes later Venkatesh asks @B to redeploy 1.9.0 in Dev. @B does not deploy. It opens run R-2207, shows its steps, and asks the right person to approve.

14:33 · the request

R-2207 is a durable run

The redeploy request becomes run R-2207, linked to the tenant, the conversation, the incoming message and the exact action. The same run appears in Vector: Approvals lists it as “Requested via @B · waiting for Sam Patel”, and Home's pipeline card shows “Deploy waiting for approval · R-2207”.

The run card

Four steps, one person

The bundle loyalty-consumer 1.9.0 (sha256:61d3…ad82) is verified. Approval by the Customer Platform owner, Sam Patel, was requested at 14:33. Roll-out on nsx-dev-use1 and the Kafka consumer group on v2 have not started. The card's footer says it plainly: “Agent-initiated deploys need a person.”

The approval

Bound to the bundle and to Dev

Sam gets the approval card in a direct message at 14:33. The approval is bound to that exact bundle and to Dev, and his permissions are checked when he clicks. If the action changes, the receipt expires; anyone else who clicks needs their own authorization. At 14:42 Maya Chen notes Sam is in a review until 15:30.

Status

Six honest states

A run card moves through accepted, working, waiting for approval, and then completed, failed or cancelled. R-2207 sits at waiting for approval in the design. Each card update comes from the persisted run state, so Teams and Vector cannot disagree.

Retries and cancellation

Once, and truthfully

Repeated message deliveries and card clicks are deduplicated. A retry after a timeout must not create a second pull request or deployment. Cancelling stops pending work it can stop and reports the effects already committed; it cannot promise to undo every external action.

Guardrails in action

When @B asks, and when it answers privately

The @B board shows two guardrails beside the main thread and states a third under them. A fourth, the recheck on follow-ups, comes from the handbook plan.

  1. Uses each person's own permissionsThe asker's identity decides what @B reads. Permissions are checked again on follow-ups, approval clicks, cached results and outgoing content.
  2. Private results go to a direct messageIf a result cannot be shown to everyone in the channel, the channel gets a minimal status and the asker gets the rest privately.
  3. Asks when a request is ambiguousWheeljack is a team, so “deploy wheeljack” needs an application, a version and an environment before anything runs.

How the prototype does it

It does not, yet

Atlas, the working prototype, has no Teams code at all. The handbook records the plan and the boundary a real bot would have to respect.

Handbook chapter 15 is marked “planned, not implemented”. The repository has no Teams route, conversation component, webhook, Adaptive Card implementation, Graph client, Teams SDK package, token handler or bot run state.

One detail can mislead a demo. In the synthetic integration catalog, 39 of the 66 cards start as configured, and Microsoft Teams is one of them. That flag says nothing about a bot; @B and real Teams connectivity are absent.

The existing plan asks for a local proof of concept: a Teams-style conversation on the same Order Service fraud scenario, a visible @B mention, a context card, animated investigation, check and evaluation steps, a proposed change, an approval state and an Open in Atlas handoff. It should also show denied access, missing context, failed checks and cancellation. Its cards would share state with Atlas's run, evaluation and release screens and stay labelled as a simulation, because an “approved” card disconnected from the release fingerprint would undermine the evidence story.

Acceptance criteria for a real integration

Handbook 15.8 lists what a real @B would have to prove before anyone relies on it.

  1. The right identityA correct mention resolves the proper user, tenant, conversation and project; unrelated messages trigger nothing.
  2. Denied stays deniedDenied or revoked repositories stay inaccessible.
  3. No leaks in previewsShared conversations never receive private source content through summaries or previews.
  4. Evidence and run IDsChecks return source-linked evidence and run IDs.
  5. Bound approvalsApprovals bind the exact action, the environment and the authorized identities.
  6. Effects happen onceDuplicate delivery cannot repeat effects.
  7. Truthful statusFailures, throttling and cancellation produce truthful status.
  8. Follow-ups reauthorizeFollow-ups keep useful context while scope is authorized again.

Production proposal

A verified adapter in front of common platform services; identity mapping and bounded context; durable runs with deduplication, safe retries and truthful cancellation; the 15.8 acceptance criteria. Open: pilot team, allowed actions, installation policy, consent, history limits, retention, audience checks and the Teams SDK.

Other ways to do it

Same question, other assistants

Chat assistants and ChatOps bots differ mostly in three places: whose permissions they use, what context they read, and who must say yes before something changes.
